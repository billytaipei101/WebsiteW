## Why Adapt the Eurotest?

The **Eurotest** (Carnero, 2005) is a brief dementia-screening tool built around a deceptively everyday task: handling money. Because it leans on numeracy and practical reasoning rather than reading or writing, it travels well across literacy levels — which matters a great deal in a country like Colombia, where a meaningful share of older adults never received formal schooling. Rather than translate the Eurotest directly, we redesigned it around Colombian currency and validated it from scratch, producing a Colombian version we called **Pesotest**.

## Adapting the Instrument

Adapting a currency-based test isn't just a matter of swapping euros for pesos. The item set, denominations, and difficulty structure all had to be rebuilt around Colombian coins and bills, and content validity was checked by a panel of expert judges before the new instrument — Pesotest — was finalized. Structurally, Pesotest mirrors the Eurotest's three-part design:

- **Part I — Denomination:** naming currently circulating coins and bills.
- **Part II — Calculation:** arithmetic tasks that involve counting, making change, and splitting a pool of coins into equal piles of matching value.
- **Part III — Recall:** after a semantic verbal-fluency task used purely as a distractor, participants recall the coins and bills handled earlier in the test.

The full test takes about 15 minutes to administer and, in most cases, needs nothing more than a handful of coins and bills — no specialized materials or computer scoring required.

## Sample and Method

Pesotest was administered to 73 adults over age 40 (mean age 67, *SD* = 12; 24% men, 76% women), drawn from all six Colombian socioeconomic strata and from 12 cities, with the largest groups from Bogotá (15%) and Ibagué (13.7%). Educational attainment ranged from no formal schooling to postgraduate degrees, and 11% of participants were functionally illiterate. Of the sample, 34% (n = 25) had a DSM-IV dementia diagnosis confirmed by treating clinicians, forming the clinical comparison group against the cognitively healthy remainder.

## Reliability and Validity

- **Internal consistency:** Cronbach's alpha was 0.72 for the Denomination subtest, 0.81 for Calculation, 0.75 for Recall, and 0.85 for the total score.
- **Convergent validity:** Pesotest correlated with the MMSE at *r* = 0.79.
- **Construct validity:** a principal-factor analysis extracted three factors explaining 62.45% of total variance, with the Calculation subtest carrying the largest share (33%) — consistent with calculation ability being one of the more sensitive markers of dementia.
- **Diagnostic accuracy:** at the recommended cutoff of 18 points (out of a maximum around 30), Pesotest reached 0.92 sensitivity and 0.84 specificity for the clinical dementia diagnosis.
- Scores on every Pesotest subtest, and on the MMSE, differed significantly between the clinical and non-clinical groups (all *p* < 0.001, independent-samples *t*), and — importantly — total scores did **not** differ by literacy status within the clinical group, supporting Pesotest's central goal of remaining fair to unschooled and illiterate adults.

## What the Item-Level Analysis Showed

A Rasch model (analyzed in Winsteps) was fit to examine how individual items behaved. Recall items — remembering how many coins of a given denomination were shown, or the total amount handled earlier — were consistently the most difficult, while denomination items (simply naming a coin or bill) were the easiest. A differential item functioning (DIF) analysis comparing the clinical and non-clinical groups found that the largest DIF values clustered in exactly those recall items: for someone with a dementia diagnosis, the probability of answering them correctly dropped close to zero, far more steeply than the difficulty gradient alone would predict. In other words, memory-for-money items don't just get harder for people with dementia — they become disproportionately, almost categorically, difficult, which is what makes them such strong diagnostic markers.

## Takeaway

Pesotest demonstrates that a short, literacy-independent, money-handling task can do real diagnostic work: strong agreement with the MMSE, high sensitivity and specificity at a simple cutoff score, and item-level evidence pointing squarely at memory encoding/retrieval — rather than calculation or naming — as the component most disrupted by dementia. Because it doesn't penalize participants for low literacy, it's a practical option for first-line cognitive screening in populations, like much of Colombia's older adult population, where educational background varies widely.
