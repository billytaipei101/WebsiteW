Poster presentation (Paper No. 07_1001) at the **5th Asian Congress of Health Psychology (ACHP 2013)**, held at the Daejeon Convention Center, Daejeon, South Korea, August 22–24, 2013. Co-authored with Hydra Ng, Yu-Siou Lin, and Yi-An Lin (Department of Psychology, National Chengchi University, Taiwan) — all four authors contributed equally, listed alphabetically.

## What the Meta-Analysis Did

The study quantitatively assessed how effective cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is for adults with chronic fatigue syndrome (CFS), searching nine databases for papers published 2002–2012. After screening for studies that both applied CBT to CFS patients and reported enough data to calculate an effect size, 22 studies qualified (combined sample of 3,176 patients, mean age 40.14). Effect sizes were computed using Lipsey and Wilson's (2001) SPSS macros.

Four questions drove the analysis: whether CBT's reported effect differs across the three most common CFS severity measures (CIS-F, CFQs, SF-36); whether within-group and between-group study designs produce different effect sizes; whether patient/study characteristics (gender, age, illness duration, publication year, treatment duration) moderate the CBT effect; and whether the year data were collected moderates it.

## Key Findings

- CBT showed a promising overall effect on CFS, but the size of that effect varied significantly depending on which of the three severity measures a study used.
- Within-group and between-group designs didn't differ significantly, though within-group studies trended toward larger effect sizes.
- Gender, age, illness duration, and treatment duration showed no moderating effect on treatment outcomes.
- Studies with data collected between 2008–2012 showed a significantly better CBT effect than those from 2002–2007 — suggesting that advances in CBT technique and understanding of CFS prognosis over that period may have improved real-world treatment effectiveness.
