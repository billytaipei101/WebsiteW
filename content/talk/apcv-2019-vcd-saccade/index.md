Poster presentation, *An Eye Movement Decision and Motor Control Experiment*, at the **15th Asia-Pacific Conference on Vision (APCV 2019)**, Osaka, Japan, July 29 – August 1, 2019. Co-authored with Tsai Jie Li and Chia Hsing Chen (Eye Movement and Reading Laboratory, Department of Psychology, National Chengchi University). Presentation supported by an allowance from the Taiwanese Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST-108-2922-I-006-154).

## The Question

Prior eye-movement research on alphabetic languages established that low-level visual cues — like word length and inter-word spacing — help readers decide where to send their eyes next (the *saccade target*). Chinese text has no inter-word spacing, so this study asked: do the visual components *within* a Chinese word carry the same kind of cue?

## The VCD Index

To test this, we devised the **Visual Complexity Distribution (VCD)** index: a measure of how a 2-character word's visual "ink" — its stroke density — is distributed across the word's horizontal extent. Words were classified as **Left-Bias** (VCD ≤ −10), **Non-Bias** (VCD between −0.09 and 0.09), or **Right-Bias** (VCD ≥ 10), with word frequency, neighborhood size, and stroke count held comparable across all three groups.

## Method

Forty-two native Chinese-speaking students (NCCU) read single-line sentences containing these target words while an EyeLink 1000 eye tracker recorded their eye movements at 1000 Hz. Each participant read 138 sentences across three counterbalanced conditions. Fixation durations (first fixation, single fixation, gaze duration) and initial landing position (ILP) within the target word were analyzed using linear mixed models.

## Findings

- Initial landing position shifted **rightward** when the eyes targeted Right-Bias words, compared to Left-Bias or Non-Bias words — meaning the within-word luminance pattern itself pulls the saccade target.
- Fixation durations (FFD, SFD, gaze duration) were significantly **shorter** for biased words (Left- or Right-Bias) than for Non-Bias words, suggesting biased luminance patterns ease early word processing.
- Launch site (where the previous saccade started from) had a large, independent effect on landing position, as expected from prior reading research — but the VCD effect held even after accounting for it.

Together, the results suggest that Chinese readers use the distribution of visual components within a word — not just word boundaries — as a cue for where to send their eyes next, and that this cue also modulates how quickly the word is processed once fixated.
